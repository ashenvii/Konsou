Four ways to make the wall an object

The structure and the turn are settled and measured. What is not settled is what this thing is made of. Right now the chit is a darker rectangle with a dashed line down it: the ticket-stub idea is written in the code and does not exist in the pixels, and the wall is a flat picture behind a scrim.

Each of these takes a different route to depth: material, housing, light, space. Pick one by letter. Then the tuner opens on the real card and the values get set there, not here.

Real size544 × 365, which is what the card measures at a 1280 viewport. Not a comp stage.

Real valuesLantern's own tokens, the 8 × 5 tile grid, veil at 0.50, and the count at the 84px the fit routine actually gives it.

Drawn, not fetchedCovers are stand-in gradients. AniList art is CORS-blocked outside the Tauri webview.

Pointer parkedThe hole and the linework are frozen at one spot so a still picture shows the lit state. One tile is frozen mid-fall.

Drawn loudEach treatment is pushed past where it would ship, so the four are telling apart on one screen. Strength is a slider, and the slider comes later.

As built, with Dropped removed from the ledger

Now

What is there today

Everything here is correct and none of it is characterful. The depth cues are real but they are all invisible until you move the mouse: parallax, the hole, the linework. Sitting still, the card is three flat rectangles.

The gap
The chit is --w-sunk at 0.94 opacity. That is a darker rectangle. Nothing about it is torn, printed, lit or lying on anything.
Also true
The wall has no near or far. Every tile is lit the same, so 40 covers read as a texture rather than as forty things.

Torn edge, cast shadow, grain, stamped ink

A

Ticket

material

Pay off the metaphor that is already in the code. The chit becomes a piece of paper torn off a roll and dropped on the wall: a real torn edge instead of a dashed border, a shadow it casts to the right, grain in the stock, and ink that sits in the paper rather than glowing off it. CLOSED becomes a rubber stamp with a boxed rule and uneven ink.

Why it is the strongest
It is the only direction where the depth is also the meaning. A torn stub is a transaction that is done, which is the whole argument for this card. Nothing else on the page is made of paper, so it also gives the archive its own material.
What it costs
Lantern only. Neon has no paper in it, so Neon needs its own answer: the shutter thickened into a real steel roller, which is the same idea in a different material.
What gets built
  • Torn edge as a generated clip path, jitter seeded so it is stable across renders
  • Drop shadow on the stub, which needs filter not box-shadow to follow the torn silhouette
  • Grain as two stacked gradients, no image asset
  • The stamp: boxed, masked for uneven ink, contrast re-measured on the chit

Recessed housing, lit top lip, everything printed mounted forward

B

Case

housing

No new material at all. The wall is set back inside a housing, with the light catching the top lip and the four inner edges falling into shadow, and everything printed sits on the front of the case with its own shadow. A faint sheen across the top-left corner says there is glass in front of the wall.

Why it might beat A
It is geometry and light, so it works identically in both worlds and needs no second answer for Neon. It also justifies the veil: a scrim over the covers stops being an effect and becomes the glass.
What it costs
Inner shadows eat the wall. The board already only shows a 304px strip of covers beside the chit, and shading four inner edges takes another ~26px off each side of that. It also gets close to a look this app has spent months avoiding, which is a card that reads as a box.
What gets built
Five inset shadows on the clip, one sheen layer, elevation on three existing elements. The smallest of the four.

One raking source, tile edges, a long shadow off the chit

C

Lamp

light

The page is already lit by one lamp above Tonight. This carries that light down onto the board: the wall is brighter at the top left and falls away to the bottom right, the chit throws a long soft shadow across the covers, every tile gets a bottom edge so it reads as a separate object, and the falling flap catches the light on its leading edge.

Why it is the safe one
It adds nothing the world does not already state. Tonight is page-lit and the board would be lit by the same source, which is the most consistent answer available and the one least likely to look stuck on.
What it costs
It may read as no change. You have a pointer lamp already; a second fixed one risks being invisible on the surface it sits on. This is the direction most at risk of you looking at it and saying it is the same card.
What gets built
One gradient layer under the veil, a tile edge, a shadow on the chit, and a specular keyframe added to the existing flap shading.

The wall turned into the card, corners pulled down, printing on the near plane

D

Depth of field

space

The wall stops being a picture on the card and becomes a space behind it. It is turned slightly away so the far edge recedes, the corners are pulled down, and the chit and the ledger sit on a near plane in front of it. A departure board seen from across a hall rather than pressed flat against the glass.

Why it is interesting
The flap already turns in perspective on a 560px vanishing point that belongs to the wall. This direction is just believing that vanishing point for the whole wall instead of only for the one tile using it.
What it costs
The riskiest of the four. A turned wall makes the tile grid non-uniform, which the linework draws as a flat lattice, so the two would disagree unless the linework turns with it. It also fights the page, where nothing else has a vanishing point.
What gets built
Perspective and rotation on the wall, a vignette, elevation on the near plane, and the linework rebuilt to sit in the same space.

My call, if you want one

A, with B's inner top lip kept. A is the only one where the depth carries the meaning instead of just adding realism, and the chit is the thing on this card that is currently pretending. B's single lit top edge is one line of CSS and it makes the card an object in both worlds, which covers the part of A that Neon cannot use.

C is the one to pick if you think the card is close and wants settling rather than character. D is the one to pick if you want the board to be the most physical thing in the app and are willing to pay for the linework rebuild.

Whichever you name, the next step is the same: it goes into the real card and the tuner opens on it, so the values come off sliders on the actual component rather than off this page. Neon gets its own pass after, because its wall being 71% covered is a layout problem no amount of character fixes.
