Sprocketed stock, perforations between tickets, the tear line at the top

A

The roll

system

Completed is a stub torn off a market roll. This is the roll it was torn from. The order runs down a continuous strip of the same stock, one ticket per title, perforated between them, sprocketed down both edges, and it runs off the bottom of the card because there are 47 of them. The top ticket carries the tear line: it is the next one to come off.

Why it is the strongest
It makes the two anchors one object seen at two moments, which is the sentence this row of the page is already trying to say. Nothing new has to be invented and nothing has to be explained: the material is already in the app and already means "a transaction that is done", so the unspent end of it means the opposite for free.
What it costs
Lantern only, like the paper it borrows. Neon needs its own answer, and the obvious one is that a shutter has slats the way a roll has perforations.
What gets built
The order block takes the board's own stock, sprockets as a repeating radial on both edges, perforations replacing the row rules, and the block bleeds to the card's edges so the strip is cut by the card rather than ending inside it.

Every cover still wrapped except the one you are about to start

B

Wrapped

concealment

The board's veil hides the covers and thins where your hand goes. This is the same device meaning the opposite thing: these are not opened yet. Each cover sits under a waxed wrap with a sheen across it, and exactly one is clear, the one the seal beside it will start.

Why it might beat A
It states the difference between the two cards rather than their kinship: over there everything is open, here nothing is. It also works in both worlds, because a wrap is a layer over art rather than a material of its own, which is exactly what let the veil work in both.
What it costs
It dims the one thing that is recognisable. The covers on this card are already 26 x 36. Wrapping four of the five leaves a card whose art you cannot read at all, and the room's own argument is that the covers are the record.
What gets built
One overlay on the cover, one exception on the first row, and a NEXT mark. The smallest of the four by a distance.

A spindle down the left, the numbers as the hole each docket hangs on

C

The spike

vernacular

The receipt spindle every stall and every kitchen has: orders go on the spike in the order they are taken and come off the top. The numbers stop being printed beside the rows and become the hole each docket hangs on, so the ordering is structural rather than stated.

Why it is interesting
It is the only one of the four that makes the card's own numbering mean something physically, and numbering is the whole reason this block exists: it is a watch order, not a list. It also survives an empty state better than A, because a bare spike still reads.
What it costs
It fights the door. A spike says the top one comes off next, but the top one here is row 1 of five out of 47, and the rest of the queue lives a tap away. It also eats 34px of the row's width, which the titles cannot spare at 375.
What gets built
The spindle as a gradient bar with a cap, the numbers reshaped into punched rings, and the rows inset past it. The width cost needs its own answer below 720.

The backlog becomes the subject, at the board's own size, in the other hue

D

The debt

typography

No material at all. Completed prints what you have spent as a 98px serial; this prints what you owe at the same weight in the other hue, and the title count drops to a caption beside the name. The row stops being an object beside a panel and becomes two ledgers facing each other: 16,914 hours closed, 226 hours owed.

Why it might be the right answer anyway
It fixes the imbalance directly instead of dressing around it, it costs nothing in either world, and it says the truest thing this card knows. A queue of 47 is abstract; nine days of your life is not.
What it costs
A fifth row, and possibly the honesty of the number. The serial needs about 70px, which is one row. And the backlog is estimated from episode counts and durations, so a figure set that large is claiming a precision the data does not have.
What gets built
A serial and a rule in the header, the count demoted, ORDER_ROWS back to 4, and the same fit routine the board's count uses so five digits cannot run out of the card.

My call, if you want one

A, and D's serial only if you decide the hours are honest enough to print that big. A is the one that makes the page say something it is already half saying: the roll and the stub torn off it are one object, and this row of the overview is the two ends of it. B is the smallest change and the weakest, because it spends its effect on hiding the covers, which this card has too little of already.

C is the one to pick if the watch ORDER is the point of this card rather than the backlog. It is the only direction that makes the numbering physical, and if you have been thinking of the order as the real subject here, it beats A on meaning.

Whichever you name, the count-versus-serial imbalance is a separate decision and it survives all four: 20px beside 98px is either deliberate, because Completed is the lit subject of that row, or it is an oversight. Say which and I will build to it.
